New World Screwworm:
making sense of uncertain locations

I lead research into what New World Screwworm (NWSW) reports can tell us about where detections occurred. Some reports identify a whole municipality instead of an exact site. I check the records, inspect detailed imagery, and use land-cover and terrain information to examine plausible locations. Simulations then help me explore how those location assumptions affect the maps and regional comparisons.

From a report to a defensible map

Start with what the location actually means.

New World Screwworm is a parasitic fly that affects livestock and other animals. Our project studies reports of its detections. To compare where those detections were reported, I first need to know what each record represents and how closely its location describes the event. A point supplied with a report may be approximate; a municipality name can cover many different kinds of land.

My research asks how the geographic picture changes when we account for that uncertainty. I combine the written records with imagery and environmental data, then test alternative assumptions about where a reported detection could fall within its reported area.

01 · Check the reports before putting them together

I collect official records and compare their dates, identifiers, reporting categories, and totals. This helps distinguish an update to an existing report from a separate event and identifies records that need further review. I keep the originals so a later question can be traced back to what the source actually said.

The completed source-review work covered 31,172 rows across 124 retained inputs. Those are records checked, not a claim that every row represents a separate detection.

02 · Inspect the places in detailed imagery

I use detailed satellite imagery to examine individual property layouts, backyards, alleyways, roads, fields, and surrounding development. At that scale, features visible around a property can provide context that a broad land-cover category misses.

This was active interpretation. I examined whether the imagery supported the model’s assumptions about the setting and access. In both reviewed examples flagged as potentially isolated, I could see roads or approaches. That gave me a concrete reason to question the flags instead of treating them as evidence that the areas were inaccessible.

03 · Narrow the possible locations using geographic evidence

For the cattle-associated reports, I used land cover and terrain to examine which parts of the reported area were plausible settings. The scenarios applied different restrictions or weights to features such as water, steep ground, dense development, and bare or sandy land. This made the environmental assumptions explicit and gave me alternatives to compare.

The reporting category mattered. A backyard livestock setting can look very different from an open farm. Most reports in this pilot were labeled “Backyard,” so I kept the farm subset separate and examined broader assumptions as exploratory scenarios. Detailed imagery helped me assess the local setting alongside those larger geographic datasets.

04 · Explore the uncertainty that remains

Environmental screening still leaves more than one possible location. I used Monte Carlo simulation: the computer repeatedly samples possible placements under a stated set of assumptions, then summarizes which broad map areas receive those placements. This lets me examine questions such as how many mapped areas could contain the reported detections. Repeating the calculation under different assumptions shows how those choices affect the result.

I also tested the calculation itself. Random sampling produces some variation even when the assumptions stay the same. I increased the number of trials until that variation met a precision standard set before the experiment. Across sixteen comparisons, the completed experiment used 64 million trials.

What did the 64 million trials establish?

One million and two million trials per comparison did not meet every registered precision check. Four million trials per comparison did. This established a tested stopping point for the calculation under the retained model.

The largest adjusted margin for the fixed probability estimates was 0.077 percentage points, below the preset 0.1-point target. In plain terms, the uncertainty introduced by the simulation’s random sampling had become small enough for the study’s chosen standard. This number concerns the calculation; the uncertainty about actual detection sites remains part of the research.

The record covers sixteen comparisons, 32 fixed probability metrics, and checks of the ranges for the number of occupied map cells. These are numerical checks under specified scenarios, not additional observed detections.

05 · Make the geographic work repeatable

I built a process that recreates the geographic datasets from the checked source files, records each step, and stops when a check fails. The outputs keep administrative boundaries, report-linked points, and reporting areas connected to their sources. This lets the team inspect how the maps were made and rebuild the same work.

The completed ArcGIS workflow has nine stages and produces 16 linked GIS layers. I checked the identifiers, map shapes, attributes, and coordinate systems, then moved the package to another folder and reopened it in a fresh process. That tested whether the delivered work could function without relying on a hidden path or an already-open session on my computer.

What I learned from the work

  • A model flag needs to be checked against the place. Roads or approaches were visible in both reviewed examples flagged as potentially isolated. Those examples showed why an access flag should prompt inspection rather than settle the interpretation.
  • The type of report changes the assumptions we should consider. The pilot’s many backyard reports made it inappropriate to treat every record as an open-pasture location. Keeping the farm subset separate preserved that distinction.
  • The geographic picture depends on the assumptions. Changing the environmental scenarios changed the modeled regional summaries. I made those choices inspectable and established a calculation-precision standard for comparing their outputs.

The contribution is a documented way to examine location uncertainty using source review, geographic evidence, and tested calculations. These steps help the team assess what a regional map can support. The simulated placements remain scenarios; they are not confirmed addresses of the original detections.

Read the scope of the imagery and scenario findings

The retained visual review covered six selected examples, including the two access flags. It was a detailed review of those examples, not a measured accuracy rate for every location. Clear imagery can reveal properties and access features even when the analytical land-cover and terrain grids are coarser.

The imagery exports did not retain acquisition dates. Annual land-cover data describe broader time periods, not necessarily the day of a reported event. Those timing differences remain part of the interpretation. The simulations explore assumptions about reported locations; they do not establish disease spread, incidence, or the true location of every report.

Supporting the research team

Keep the evidence and decisions connected.

I also designed the system the team uses to organize assignments, submit work, review evidence, and retain decisions. I used Research Architect to generate the project’s coordination system. It supports the research described above and keeps each contribution connected to its sources and review history.

The diagram follows a contribution through the team. A contributor and their assistant form a Pod. The Hub coordinates the project, the Ingestor checks submitted work and reruns agreed steps, and the Library holds material accepted for shared use.

How work becomes shared evidence

  1. The contributor reviews their assistant’s work before submission.
  2. The Hub’s human reviewer examines the submission and decides what checking is needed.
  3. The Ingestor verifies the files and reproduces the agreed steps, then returns the results and any issues to the Hub.
  4. The Hub makes the final human acceptance decision. Only the Hub publishes accepted additions or corrections to the Library.
  5. Team members and their assistants can retrieve the approved material for later work, along with its sources and use limits.

How the project builds on earlier work

The shared record retains findings, corrected assumptions, rejected interpretations, and unanswered questions. Later work can use that history when it retrieves the relevant material. This is accumulated project context; it does not retrain the underlying AI models or guarantee that an assistant has applied every correction.

The separate map application

I independently built the Spatial Evidence Workbench to inspect records geographically, investigate selected material, and export work with its source context. The Hub’s simpler HTML evidence interface handles submissions and review history. The Workbench has its own case study so the research and the software can each be examined in detail.

For a closer technical review

Check how the work was preserved and delivered.

I built file checks, saved working states, and recovery tools so the team can identify the version it is using and inspect what happened during a handoff. These records concern the research infrastructure; they are separate from the interpretation of the location scenarios.

How source files, versions, and corrections stay traceable

Each submitted package lists the expected files and their SHA-256 fingerprints. Comparing those fingerprints detects changes or missing files. Separate records identify what was approved, sent, and received.

The Library keeps exact versions, source references, related files, corrections, and review history together. Its search distinguishes material available for use from material that must be requested from the Hub. A saved checkpoint records the project’s working state so a later session can recover it.

Long source records are delivered in complete, manageable pages. The system checks that the pieces reconstruct the original text. It also checks whether the starting checkpoint changed before publishing an update, which prevents an older session from silently replacing newer work.

Inspect the installed update and what the checks establish

Twelve targeted checks, an independent agent review of the exact files, and installed checks passed. A Unicode test reconstructed 297,516 characters across 188 pages. A real Library lookup retained all 11 sources; a message-journal fixture recovered its full 14,419-character summary instead of the earlier 640-character excerpt. Reading continuation pages triggered no new scan.

The installation preserved the active Hub, existing approvals and closed work orders, and all 24 protected file hashes. It improved evidence delivery without republishing the Library or replacing contributor kits. Extra deduplication produced no additional reduction on the already compacted Hub context, so the existing reader stayed in place.
