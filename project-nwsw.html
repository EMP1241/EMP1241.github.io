01 / Research case · Team leadership & GIS

New World Screwworm

I lead a Texas A&M student research team conducting geographic analysis for the U.S. Department of Agriculture’s New World Screwworm (NWSW) project. Our study spans Guatemala, Mexico, and the southern United States. I make incomplete and conflicting records usable for research: recover missing dates, connect livestock statistics to the right places, reconstruct transportation records, and test the geographic assumptions behind our maps. I also review and integrate the team’s research and build the software that keeps its evidence and decisions connected.

The research and my contribution

Build a geographic picture the evidence can support.

New World Screwworm is a parasitic fly that affects livestock and other animals. Our project investigates where detections have been reported, how their reported distribution has changed, and what evidence supports competing explanations. We examine detection records alongside livestock production, transportation, environmental conditions, and the coverage of surveillance.

Before those comparisons are useful, the records need to make sense together. An animal count, an outbreak report, a market total, and a surveillance location describe different things. A date may mean the start of an outbreak, its confirmation, or publication. A point may represent a broad area. I investigate those differences and build the research datasets and methods needed to work with them.

The sections below describe work I carried out or led. I also review and integrate colleagues’ contributions; the original researchers, contributors, and publishing agencies retain credit for their material.

1. Recover the history behind the reports

Resolve records that appear to disagree

We had reports from different sources and different versions of the same source. Combining their totals without checking them could count an update as a new event, treat repeated reporting as independent evidence, or mix animals with outbreaks.

I collected official records, saved their original files, and compared identifiers, dates, reporting categories, totals, and location descriptions. I checked whether records referred to the same underlying report and documented conflicts that the sources could not resolve. One completed source comparison examined 31,172 entries across 124 saved source files and inputs.

This gave the team a record of which comparisons were supported, where sources overlapped, and which questions remained open. That entry count describes material checked; repeated reporting and different counting units mean it cannot be read as a count of unique detections.

Examples of what the source checks caught

Two representations of the same U.S. roster agreed on the checked fields, so they could not be treated as independent corroboration. Other source checks distinguished animal records from a fly-trap record and preserved a conflict between attributed accounts of Guatemala’s first confirmation date.

I also checked published totals against their component figures and separated outbreak-start, notification, confirmation, and publication dates. Original source differences remain visible rather than being silently replaced with a single interpretation.

Recover 2,629 missing outbreak-start dates

In a separate reference dataset, 2,629 records had blank date fields. They could appear in the overall geographic view, but the missing dates prevented their use in the same time-window comparisons as dated records.

I traced their record identifiers back to official public records and recovered the outbreak-start dates. I then checked the recovered dates against retained month labels and tested the matching process against records whose dates were already known. All 45 previously dated comparison records agreed after converting their timestamps to the same time standard.

The recovery filled all 2,629 blanks in that dataset: 2,604 Mexico records and 25 U.S. records. That supplied the missing timing information for placing those records in the outbreak-start timeline. Each recovered value remains traceable to its supporting record.

What the date recovery resolves

The matches used exact record identifiers, and no uncertain date matches remained in this dataset. The values describe official outbreak starts; confirmation and notification dates answer different questions. This repair addresses timing. Location accuracy and overlap between related reporting sources still require their own checks.

2. Make livestock and transportation evidence comparable

Connect livestock figures to the correct places

Livestock tables and geographic boundaries did not always use the same names, area codes, or boundary versions. Inventory and production figures also measured different things. These mismatches could leave a value unmatched or associate it with the wrong geographic area.

I continued the livestock-data research, connected records to documented boundaries, and investigated flagged production rows. I checked geographic names and codes, preserved the year and unit attached to each value, and compared municipal production tables with the publisher’s yearbook figures.

The completed work connected 18,021 records to geographic areas and resolved 72 flagged production rows across 18 issues. All 462 yearbook comparisons agreed exactly or within rounding. The team received geographically usable data with a record of how discrepancies were handled, rather than a table whose map locations and units had to be assumed.

How I checked the livestock-data connections

The work integrated 13 boundary layers. All 2,456 rows in the retained Mexico inventory table and all 1,524 Texas inventory rows matched their documented area identifiers. Recent production records were checked separately against their applicable geographic codes, with naming differences retained.

Of the 462 production comparisons, 456 matched exactly and six differed only within rounding. These were consistency checks between products from the same publisher. They do not turn a historical cattle inventory into a current count or establish an independently measured disease rate. Earlier contributor source and review credit is preserved.

Reconstruct scattered cattle-transport records

The transportation evidence was spread across weekly reports, older reporting series, trade summaries, and market records. Some periods were missing, some series overlapped, and automated table extraction had left values blank even where the original reports printed zero.

I expanded the collection, recovered available report periods, checked extracted tables against the saved original reports, and consolidated the records with their source references. The October 17 collection included 189 national weekly reports and 25 legacy reports, alongside trade and market series.

Re-extracting the weekly reports confirmed 1,698 numeric cells and recovered explicit printed zeros from 1,728 fields previously left blank by extraction. This distinguished “the source reported none” from “the value is missing.” I also documented overlapping series so the same reported activity would not be added twice.

The result is a structured record the team can use to compare reported transport and market activity over time, while seeing where coverage ends or a count means something different.

What the transportation evidence can support

Published border-gateway counts, auction totals, inventory, production, issued documents, and modeled trips remain separate measures. Country-to-gateway figures can describe reported activity at that scale; they do not identify a receiving ranch or reconstruct every animal’s journey.

Missing report periods remain missing. The work preserves original documents, extracted values, reporting windows, and source references. It does not establish that a particular shipment caused a detection. Contributor-origin analysis and institutional source credit remain attached to the material.

3. Check environmental evidence before drawing comparisons

Test whether weather records can be used together

A weather dataset may contain a number for every map location while still differing from local observations. The records can also disagree about when a “day” begins and ends. Before connecting environmental information to the research, I checked coverage, timing, and the suitability of the available measurements.

I checked 85,536 saved weather entries, refreshed 15 station sources, and compared temperature estimates with station observations on dates covered by both. After checking which observations were suitable, the comparison contained 166 station-days at six sites: a station-day is one day of observations at one station.

The comparison documented differences among sites and an unresolved discrepancy involving one station. I retained the discrepancy and its possible explanations in the report. This gave the team a bounded assessment of where the temperature comparison was usable and what still needed investigation.

What was checked, and what remained open

I examined daily timing conventions separately and repeated comparisons on common dates. Some stations lacked observations suitable for the comparison. Partial rainfall totals were not stretched into full-day measurements, and missing values were not treated as zero.

This was a selected-site temperature diagnostic. Whether the findings held across the wider region, whether humidity and full-day rainfall estimates agreed with observations, and whether weather was associated with detections remained open. My acquisition and checking work builds on environmental preparation contributed by a teammate, whose original credit is retained.

Evaluate a proposed way to fill missing rainfall

Missing rainfall observations left a gap in the environmental evidence. Before relying on an estimate, I tested reconstruction approaches against historical periods where observed values were available, so the estimated and observed amounts could be compared.

The final evaluation covered 18 distinct historical months. I retained poor examples, checked the arithmetic, and documented the method’s performance on days whose observations were deliberately left out, then used to check the estimates. Some comparisons performed poorly, including a test where the method’s average error was greater than simply assuming no rain.

I prepared and delivered the examination package with those adverse results intact. The team could inspect the tested method, its weak results, and the reasons the estimate remained provisional before deciding whether to use it.

Why this evaluation did not establish general rainfall accuracy

The historical months were selected for usable observation coverage. That selection and the differences between historical and target periods limit how broadly the results apply. The evaluation did not establish representative accuracy for the target period or automatic suitability for later analyses.

4. Investigate what the map leaves uncertain

Inspect individual settings in detailed imagery

Some reports name a municipality without identifying the actual detection site. I inspected detailed satellite imagery to examine individual property layouts, backyard settings, alleyways, roads, fields, and surrounding development. Those visible features provide local context that broader land-cover data can miss.

I used that inspection to question the model’s interpretation of a place. In both reviewed examples flagged as potentially isolated, I could see roads or approaches. That changed how the flags should be read: they were questions to investigate, not evidence that the locations were inaccessible.

I also combined imagery with land-cover and terrain information to narrow plausible locations under the scenarios being tested. Water, steep ground, dense development, and bare or sandy land received different treatment depending on the assumptions. I kept the reporting categories visible and analyzed farm reports separately; most reports in the pilot were labeled “Backyard.”

Compare the geographic consequences of different assumptions

Screening still leaves multiple possible locations. I developed Monte Carlo simulations, which repeatedly sample possible placements under a stated set of assumptions. The outputs summarize which broad map areas receive those placements and how many areas could contain the reported detections.

Changing the environmental scenarios changed the modeled regional summaries. This let me examine how much of the geographic picture depended on choices about unknown locations, rather than presenting one assumed placement as the only answer.

I also expanded beyond the initial pilot to the saved records that could be represented geographically across the study region. That separate regional experiment completed 12 million simulation trials and passed its specified numerical checks. It preserved differences in source coverage and geographic detail, including records that identified only a larger area, such as a department.

Determine when the simulation had run enough trials

Even with unchanged assumptions, random sampling introduces variation into a calculation. I ran a separate precision experiment to find how many trials were needed to keep that variation below the study’s preset target. This helps distinguish a change caused by the geographic assumptions from ordinary sampling noise in the computation.

Across sixteen comparisons, the completed experiment used 64 million trials. One million and two million trials per comparison did not meet every check; four million did. That established a tested stopping point for this calculation.

Read the numerical result and the scope of both experiments

The largest adjusted margin for the fixed probability estimates was 0.077 percentage points, below the 0.1-point target. This measures uncertainty introduced by random sampling in the calculation. It does not measure the distance between a simulated location and a real detection site.

The 64-million-trial precision experiment and the 12-million-trial regional expansion were separate experiments. Their trial counts are not observations or new detections. The wider regional expansion retained its own geographic assumptions; it did not apply the pilot’s environmental narrowing to every record. Overlap between related reporting sources remained unresolved, so their combined entry count was not presented as a count of separate events.

Read the scope of the imagery findings

The retained visual review covered six selected examples, including the two access flags. Detailed imagery can reveal properties and access features even when the analytical land-cover and terrain grids are coarser. This review was not a measured accuracy rate for every location.

The imagery exports did not retain acquisition dates. Annual land-cover data describe broader time periods rather than the exact day of an event. Those timing differences remain part of the interpretation. Simulated placements are possible locations under stated assumptions. These simulations do not establish verified detection-site addresses or a cause of spread.

5. Make the geographic work rebuildable

A map delivered on its own can be difficult to inspect or reproduce. I developed a workflow that recreates the geographic datasets from checked source files, records each step, and stops when a required check fails. Administrative boundaries, report-linked points, and reporting areas remain connected to their sources.

One completed ArcGIS workflow has nine stages producing 16 connected GIS layers. I checked record identifiers, descriptive fields, map shapes, and the coordinate systems used to place them. I then moved the package to another folder and reopened it in a fresh process to check that the delivery did not depend on a hidden path or an already-open session.

This leaves the team a documented route from the retained records to the geographic outputs. A reviewer can examine how the work was produced, and a later research session can rebuild it rather than start from an unexplained map.

What these contributions changed

  • Previously undated records gained traceable outbreak-start dates for use in chronological comparisons.
  • Livestock figures gained documented geographic matches, and flagged production discrepancies were resolved.
  • Scattered transport reports became a structured collection with extracted values, reporting periods, explicit zeros, and coverage gaps retained.
  • Environmental methods were examined before use, including results showing where estimates were weak or unresolved.
  • Geographic assumptions became inspectable and testable, with repeatable map-building work and numerical checks behind the simulations.

These are completed contributions to the research foundation and its methods. The team’s integrated assessment continues to evaluate competing explanations and identify what additional evidence would most reduce uncertainty.

6. Lead and integrate the team’s research

I define and coordinate research tasks, investigate gaps, review submissions, request corrections, and integrate contributions into the wider assessment. That includes checking whether a conclusion follows from its sources, whether apparently separate reports share the same origin, and whether a proposed dataset is suitable for the question being asked.

I turn review findings into specific follow-up work: clarify a date, recover an original report, resolve a mismatch, repeat a calculation, or explain the limits of a conclusion. Reviewed contributions retain their author, sources, methods, and accepted use. My own analyses remain distinguishable from work I reviewed or integrated.

I also built the system below to keep those decisions and corrections available to the team, so later work can use the project’s accumulated evidence and review history.

7. Build the system supporting the research

Keep sources, contributions, and decisions connected.

Research contributions arrive as files, analyses, and supporting records. I designed the system the team uses to organize that work, submit it for review, track corrections, and retain decisions. I used Research Architect to generate the project’s coordination system. It supports the research described above and keeps each contribution connected to its sources and review history.

The diagram follows a contribution through the team. A contributor and their assistant form a Pod. The Hub coordinates the project, the Ingestor checks submitted work and reruns agreed steps, and the Library holds material accepted for shared use.

How work becomes shared evidence

  1. The contributor reviews their assistant’s work before submission.
  2. The Hub’s human reviewer examines the submission and decides what checking is needed.
  3. The Ingestor verifies the files and reproduces the agreed steps, then returns the results and any issues to the Hub.
  4. The Hub makes the final human acceptance decision. Only the Hub publishes accepted additions or corrections to the Library.
  5. Team members and their assistants can retrieve the approved material for later work, along with its sources and use limits.

How the project builds on earlier work

The shared record retains findings, corrected assumptions, rejected interpretations, and unanswered questions. Later work can use that history when it retrieves the relevant material. This is accumulated project context; it does not retrain the underlying AI models or guarantee that an assistant has applied every correction.

The separate map application

I independently built the Spatial Evidence Workbench to inspect records geographically, investigate selected material, and export work with its source context. The Hub’s simpler HTML evidence interface handles submissions and review history. The Workbench has its own case study so the research and the software can each be examined in detail.

For a closer technical review

Check how the work was preserved and delivered.

I built file checks, saved working states, and recovery tools so the team can identify the version it is using and inspect what happened during a handoff. These records concern the research infrastructure; they are separate from the interpretation of the location scenarios.

How source files, versions, and corrections stay traceable

Each submitted package lists the expected files and their SHA-256 fingerprints. Comparing those fingerprints detects changes or missing files. Separate records identify what was approved, sent, and received.

The Library keeps exact versions, source references, related files, corrections, and review history together. Its search distinguishes material available for use from material that must be requested from the Hub. A saved checkpoint records the project’s working state so a later session can recover it.

Long source records are delivered in complete, manageable pages. The system checks that the pieces reconstruct the original text. It also checks whether the starting checkpoint changed before publishing an update, which prevents an older session from silently replacing newer work.

Inspect the installed update and what the checks establish

Twelve targeted checks, an independent agent review of the exact files, and installed checks passed. A Unicode test reconstructed 297,516 characters across 188 pages. A real Library lookup retained all 11 sources; a message-journal fixture recovered its full 14,419-character summary instead of the earlier 640-character excerpt. Reading continuation pages triggered no new scan.

The installation preserved the active Hub, existing approvals and closed work orders, and all 24 protected file hashes. It improved evidence delivery without republishing the Library or replacing contributor kits. Extra deduplication produced no additional reduction on the already compacted Hub context, so the existing reader stayed in place.
