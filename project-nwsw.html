The question behind the maps

How do reporting practices, surveillance coverage, and changing map boundaries affect what the available New World Screwworm records can tell us? I bring sources into a common geographic and analytical framework so differences in reporting are not mistaken for differences in the underlying biology.

My role

I lead GIS development and the team’s research coordination: define evidence needs, reconcile reporting definitions, prepare reusable geography, connect records to the areas they describe, and integrate contributions into a shared comparison table. I build and maintain the software and delivery controls that keep source identities, method choices, contributor attribution, and unresolved gaps attached to the work.

From Research Architect to the project Hub

Research Architect is the reusable framework that turns a reviewed plan into individual AI assistant kits and shared research controls. I used it to generate the NWSW Hub, the project-specific system for assignments, incoming work, checkpoints, review, and handoffs. A checkpoint is a saved working state that a later session can recover.

The framework’s small HTML evidence interface lets the team inspect submissions, revisions, receipts, and originals. It is separate from the Spatial Evidence Workbench, the independent GIS application I built for mapped investigations and exports.

Keeping a growing research history usable

As the history grew, I added bounded context retrieval: a large lookup is delivered in manageable pages that retain the complete underlying record and its source references. The system checks that the parts reconstruct the original content. Publication also checks whether its starting checkpoint changed while work was underway, so an older session cannot silently publish over a newer state.

Each contribution travels with a versioned manifest—a list of expected files—and SHA-256 fingerprints that flag changed contents. Intake checks identities and sizes, preserves conflicting versions, and records a receipt. Technical checks establish which files arrived; human review determines how the research enters the shared assessment.

A GIS workflow another person can rebuild

I built a scripted nine-stage ArcGIS workflow that reconstructs 16 linked GIS layers from a fixed, hash-checked input package. These layers include administrative boundaries, source-linked points, and separately categorized reporting areas; they are not 16 independent research findings. The build checks the licensed software environment, writes a log for every stage, stops if a stage fails, and confirms that the frozen inputs stayed unchanged.

Checks cover stable identifiers, crosswalks that connect source labels to geographic areas, valid geometry, expected attributes, and coordinate systems. Fresh map exports are inspected, and the package is reopened from a relocated folder in a new process. That checks whether the deliverable depends on a hidden path or state on my computer.

What the verification establishes

Inspect the October 16–17 engineering evidence

The context-retrieval repair reproduced two failures, then checked 163 existing source-navigation pointers and 165 pointers in the published successor. These pointers locate research context and sources; they are not transportation routes. Complete text reconstruction, bounded pages, retained history, and unchanged controls were checked separately.

The October 17 local ArcGIS Pro 3.7.3 rebuild completed all nine scripted stages and passed the recorded data, geometry, attribute, coordinate-system, export, and relocation checks. Exact package delivery was verified. Another researcher’s independent rerun and the scientific interpretation remain separate review steps.

The research and cross-team synthesis remain in progress. These engineering checks support traceable preparation and delivery; they do not validate biological claims or establish that another person has reproduced the analysis.
