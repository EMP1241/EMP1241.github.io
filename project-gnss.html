The question

A small accuracy radius can look convincing on a screen. I investigated whether device-reported uncertainty tracked observed horizontal error as pedestrian crowd conditions changed.

From class project to independent research

The work began as a GNSS class project. I continued it independently, more than doubled the original field collection, developed the analysis, and carried the findings into an updated research poster and spatial scenario.

Classmates contributed to the original class-stage field collection. I led the expanded collection, analysis, interpretation, and continued research.

Data and method

The updated study contains 781 retained readings from three smartphones across five crowd categories. ArcGIS Field Maps recorded positions relative to an NGS-published reference. I structured and analyzed the observations in Python, comparing measured error with reported uncertainty using Welch’s ANOVA and adjusted regression models.

The deception gap

In the 41+ people category, the iPhone 13 averaged 109.4 feet of actual horizontal error while reporting 16.7 feet of uncertainty. The discrepancy shows why a device’s reported precision and its observed positional error need to be examined separately.

This is a single-site observational result. Repeated readings and reference uncertainty constrain its interpretation; the experiment does not directly test E911-delivered positions.

Making the scale visible

I extended the communication of the findings with a hypothetical present-day New Orleans layout. Thirty synthetic reported positions and overlapping 171-foot modeled buffers form an approximately 50-acre footprint. The layout helps a viewer understand the area behind a set of precise-looking points.

The green points and red combined area are illustrative. The scenario is not a reconstruction of historical Hurricane Katrina calls. Both original QR links remain in the updated poster.
