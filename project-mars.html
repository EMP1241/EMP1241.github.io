From mission archives to a place you can explore

Curiosity rover positions, photographs, orbital elevation and imagery, and instrument readings are stored in separate NASA archives. Mars Explorer links those records in one spatial workspace, so visitors can move between a reconstructed site and the source material behind it. It is a set of selected Gale Crater locations, not a complete 3D model of Mars.

My role

I lead the five-person project team and design and implement the spatial database, source-processing pipelines, data service, and interactive 3D terrain system. I connect the terrain and interface to source-linked science records, and maintain the project’s original interface and integrations.

Building the Martian surface

My terrain pipeline turns elevation grids and orbital imagery into surfaces for Cesium, the 3D globe and terrain viewer. Python, NumPy, Rasterio, and GDAL prepare the elevation samples and triangular surface geometry. Image texture coordinates align each pixel to the terrain; surface normals tell the renderer how to shade it. Separate grid transforms preserve the alignment between elevation and imagery.

At Rocknest, elevation samples are spaced 1 meter apart and imagery uses a 0.25-meter grid where the source products are available. These are data-sample spacings, not measures of accuracy. The terrain uses custom Cesium geometry; movement stays within the prepared area. The Rocknest walkthrough starts at a synthetic 1.7-meter viewpoint, not a reconstructed rover camera location.

A database beneath the landscape

A relational database of 41 connected tables keeps rover positions, science observations, archive products, and exact source files in separate but linked records. PostgreSQL/PostGIS stores the records and their locations; a read-only FastAPI service makes them available to the interface and database browser.

A rover location used for context and an instrument observation may come from different sols (Martian days). The database preserves each source time and coordinate frame—the reference system used to locate a point—rather than merging them into one timestamp. It also retains the precision reported by each source; reported precision does not by itself establish absolute accuracy.

What the prototype does

Visitors can explore records for Rocknest, Yellowknife Bay, Murray Buttes, and Mont Mercou, inspect rover photographs, and open the regional 3D terrain packages that are available. The database browser shows connected tables, records, field types, and links between them. At Rocknest, it links APXS measurements to their source; at Yellowknife Bay, it retains a separately sourced rover study-station record.

What the visuals represent

The terrain is reconstructed from orbital elevation and image data. Rover photographs and mosaics provide separate views, including an interactive Navcam panorama at Mont Mercou. The panorama can be panned, but it is not registered to the 3D terrain or presented as a reconstructed rover-camera position. The next step is to add observation packages with documented location and time associations.
