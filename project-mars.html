Mars Explorer

I lead a five-person team building a way to explore places visited by NASA’s Curiosity rover. I developed the coordinate handling, spatial database, and terrain-processing workflow that connect rover positions, orbital images, elevation measurements, and scientific records. Mars Explorer brings those sources into one application where people can explore selected landscapes and inspect the mission evidence behind them.

From mission archives to a place you can explore

Curiosity’s rover positions, photographs, orbital imagery, elevation measurements, and instrument readings are distributed across technical archives and separate viewers. I built Mars Explorer to connect those records through places people can explore. My motivation was to help people feel connected to a distant landscape while making its scientific sources accessible. The application covers selected locations in Gale Crater.

My role

I lead the five-person project team and design and implement the spatial database, source-processing pipelines, data service, and interactive 3D terrain system. I connect the terrain and interface to source-linked science records, and maintain the project’s original interface and integrations.

The coordinate problem I had to solve

Working with geographic data on Earth is one thing. Learning how NASA’s mission records describe locations, and building the tools to turn those records into an explorable Martian landscape, is another.

I worked through the mission documentation to establish what each position and height value represented. I configured the viewer around a documented Mars reference globe and preserved the mission’s coordinate conventions. The database records what those coordinates mean, so angular values are not silently treated as distances in meters or assigned an Earth reference system.

I also separated the planet-wide overview from the close-up terrain. The ground view uses local east, north, and up coordinates, with source elevations measured relative to a documented local origin. This lets the application render a supported terrain area and place its viewing height consistently within that area.

Inspect the coordinate choices and remaining alignment work

The mission position records use planetocentric latitude and east-positive longitude. The overview computes positions on a radius-3,396,190-meter reference sphere. PostGIS stores the angular points with SRID 0 and separately recorded coordinate definitions, rather than claiming Earth geography or meter distances.

The local surface uses MOLA-areoid elevations, Mars’s gravity-based height reference, relative to a local origin. That is separate from the overview sphere and the viewer’s synthetic eye height. Converting those source heights onto the overview sphere and physically registering rover-camera imagery to the terrain remain separate work; the current viewer does not claim either has been completed.

Building the Martian surface

The elevation grid and orbital image did not share the same pixel spacing or starting position. I built the processing that calculates where each elevation sample falls within the image’s own grid, including fractional offsets. A simple scale-up would not establish that alignment. I then used those samples to construct the triangular surface, apply the image to it, and calculate the surface directions used for shading. Python, NumPy, Rasterio, and GDAL prepare the geometry and image for Cesium, the 3D viewer.

At Rocknest, elevation samples are spaced 1 meter apart and imagery uses a 0.25-meter grid where source products are available. These are sample spacings, not accuracy measures. Across the prepared areas, ground-level exploration uses a synthetic 1.7-meter eye height, with an optional elevated view. The viewer keeps movement inside the supported terrain area. Its viewing position is synthetic; it does not reproduce a calibrated rover-camera position.

A database beneath the landscape

I designed the spatial database to connect a location with its mission records without collapsing different observations into one event. Its 41 connected tables retain rover positions, science observations, archive products, exact source files, and the relationships between them. I implemented the PostgreSQL/PostGIS storage and read-only FastAPI service that supply those records to the interface and database browser.

A rover position does not automatically locate its camera or a nearby science target. The position and measurement can also come from different sols, or Martian days. I kept their observation times, reference systems, and source-reported precision distinct while recording the supported connection between them. This allows a visitor to inspect the association without being given a falsely exact location or time.

What the prototype does

Visitors can explore Rocknest, Yellowknife Bay, Murray Buttes, and Mont Mercou, inspect source records, and open the prepared terrain packages. The database includes source-linked APXS observations at Rocknest and the Flaherty target at Yellowknife Bay. Observation time, reported precision, study-station context, and source products remain separate rather than being treated as one exactly located event.

Keep your place while exploring the evidence

The latest desktop checks covered all four areas, all twelve rover-image views, and the project’s 32 listed chemistry values. Opening a source and returning now preserves the reader’s position, keyboard focus, and open details. I also checked first launch, restart, shutdown, and moving the stopped application to a different folder on a physical USB drive.

Inspect the October 23 desktop and USB checks

The candidate-12 record lists 563 guarded unit tests, 45 companion checks, and 30 native lifecycle checks. These cover different software layers. The physical USB checks exercised first launch, browser opening, restart, confirmed shutdown, moving the stopped folder, and restarting from the new location on this host. They do not establish performance on another computer.

What the visuals represent

The current preview offers twelve curated native Navcam views—three for each of the four areas—with source selection, Previous/Next controls, and acquisition or station context. These views are separate from the reconstructed terrain; some share frames or come from a nearby station. Physically registering rover imagery to the surface requires additional evidence.

The October 23 desktop stability work is complete on the development PC. Another-computer testing, actual screen-reader checks, and further graphics-resource checks remain open. The recorded demo shows the local application; there is no public hosted app or download to link yet.
