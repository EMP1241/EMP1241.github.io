Making complex collections usable

Tree rings preserve annual growth measurements, often across decades or centuries. Bringing collections together requires more than opening their files: a measured ring width and a published chronology index are different quantities, units may need supporting evidence, and site descriptions can be incomplete or conflicting.

I built the import and review workflow that makes these files usable together. It reads different tree-ring formats, keeps widths separate from chronology indexes, checks the evidence for measurement units, and exposes missing or conflicting metadata before permanent installation. Researchers can inspect records together while retaining the distinctions that determine what their numbers mean.

My role and research impact

After demonstrating TRAE to Dr. Max C. A. Torbenson, I joined his lab as a paid research assistant in June 2026. I created the application and continue developing its research tools and data-format add-ons.

I developed and applied its workflow to derive summaries, review and organize tree-ring study data, and export prepared records for a co-authored manuscript now in preparation. The software and prepared research materials are tangible outputs; the manuscript’s findings remain ongoing research.

Maps and graphs that work together

I built the links between site selection, map locations, plots, and summaries so a researcher can move from a place to its growth record without assembling separate views. I preserved total, earlywood, and latewood widths and their units, and kept chronology indexes and variants distinct from raw measurements. The charts retain those distinctions; display smoothing does not alter the underlying values.

Linked charts show how tree-growth records compare through time, which years overlap, and where growth and climate measurements vary together. These are exploratory tools that help researchers decide what to investigate next; the statistical interpretation still requires review.

Read the statistical measures and their limits

Researchers can compare records, inspect which years each series covers, and screen for climate associations. A site mean is the year-by-year average across available tree series. LWa is a simple same-year model residual used to screen how latewood relates to earlywood; it does not identify a proven biological cause. Expressed Population Signal (EPS) estimates how consistently the available raw tree series share a common pattern; here it is a screening measure, not validation of a standard chronology. Pearson’s correlation measures linear co-variation; it cannot show that climate caused a growth change. These exploratory screens do not adjust for all time-series dependence or multiple comparisons, so chronology development and climate interpretation require further research.

The feature add-ons behind the workflow

I developed Climate Builder for the preparation work between monthly observations and a meaningful growth–climate comparison. It defines which months form a season, which year the season belongs to, and whether incomplete seasons are allowed. I also implemented sampling of PRISM climate grids at tree-site locations, retaining the source and settings behind each prepared table.

I built companion tools to inspect variables and supported annual series in NetCDF files, package existing ROXAS wood-anatomy measurement tables for R and MATLAB, and organize year-labeled WalDendro X-ray density blocks. The YEAR tool identifies missing or duplicate years and writes a reviewable copy while preserving the original measurements. ROXAS Prep works with already-produced measurements; the NetCDF companion has a defined inspection scope rather than being a general raster-analysis suite.

I added an optional local AI helper for import-review text, with checks that each proposed clue actually appears in the supplied row and field. A researcher decides whether to change metadata; the helper cannot edit measurement values.

Reviewed imports and reusable work

Temporary imports support quick inspection; reviewed managed imports add reusable library records. Parser warnings, measurement units, identity conflicts, and missing metadata are exposed for review. Original files and import records make the source traceable; SHA-256 fingerprints can reveal whether a file’s bytes changed, but they do not establish that its measurements are scientifically correct.

The offline-first local workspace separates temporary inspection from managed library installation. Reviewed imports retain source identity, measurement-scale evidence, and chronology variants; inventory and export-audit views expose missing metadata before handoff. A local helper supports guarded library writes, diagnostics, and backup/restore. External basemaps and data sources have their own connection requirements.

From inspection to analysis

I built the handoff from visual inspection to downstream analysis. Selected sites and climate records can be packaged with data tables, geographic information, source references, units, method settings, and loader scripts for R, Python, or MATLAB. The next analysis receives a documented starting point instead of a CSV whose preparation has to be reconstructed.

I also developed shareable .TRAE session files to retain the current view, temporary imports, climate choices, selected sites, and notes. The managed research library remains separate from that saved session.
